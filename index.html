How to read this

Page 3 Earth's Heated Blanket

The greenhouse effect is natural and vital for life. Sunlight warms the surface, the surface sends heat back up, and greenhouse gases absorb some of it and send it back out in all directions, including down.

  • NASA Science, Global Warming primary source archived 2026-09-20

The numbered diagram is my own drawing.

Without the greenhouse effect, Earth's average temperature would be about 0 °F.

  • NASA Science, Global Warming primary source archived 2026-09-20

NASA gives −18 °C (0 °F), compared with today's 15 °C (59 °F).

Page 4 The Key Players

About 99.57% of the atmosphere is nitrogen, oxygen, and argon. About 0.39% is water vapor and about 0.04% is carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases.

  • NOAA JetStream, The Atmosphere (composition of dry air, CO₂ at 0.042%) primary source archived 2026-09-17
  • Wikipedia, Atmosphere of Earth (water vapor averages about 0.4% over the whole atmosphere) secondary source archived 2026-09-30

The exact figures on the page are my arithmetic from those two inputs. Water vapor swings a lot with weather, so read them as averages. The 10,000-dot grid uses the same numbers (9,957, 39, and 4).

Water vapor is the most plentiful greenhouse gas and a key part of the natural warming effect. Nature produces some greenhouse gases too, but human activity has added extra.

  • NASA Science, Global Warming primary source archived 2026-09-20

Page 5 What's Changing?

The right-hand side of the CO₂ chart, from 1958 on, is the Keeling Curve, measured on Mauna Loa in Hawaii.

  • NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory, Trends in CO2 primary source archived 2026-09-29

The earlier years come from ice cores, which hold bubbles of ancient air from Antarctica.

  • NOAA NCEI Paleoclimatology, Law Dome 2000-year ice core CO₂ record primary source
  • Rubino et al. (2019), Law Dome record, CSIRO dataset (DOI) primary source

For most of the last thousand years, CO₂ stayed around 280 ppm.

  • Law Dome ice core data (see above) primary source

My calculation. The average of the Law Dome measurements from the years 1000 to 1799 is 279.6 ppm (range 271 to 284).

Today it's about 430 ppm, and the curve has been getting steeper for the past 70 years.

  • NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory, Trends in CO2 primary source archived 2026-09-29
  • UK Met Office, Mauna Loa CO₂ forecast for 2026 primary source archived 2026-09-21

NOAA's September 2026 monthly mean is 426.07 ppm, which is the yearly low. The seasonally adjusted value is 429.55 ppm and the May 2026 peak was about 432 ppm. The chart's last point is the seasonally adjusted value.

2024 was about 1.5 °C warmer than the late 1800s, and temperatures have climbed quickly since the 1970s.

  • NASA GISS, GISTEMP v4 (global temperature) primary source archived 2026-09-28
  • NASA, 2024 Was the Warmest Year on Record primary source archived 2026-09-28

My calculation. NASA reports 2024 at +1.28 °C against its 1951 to 1980 baseline. The 1880 to 1899 average sits 0.224 °C below that baseline, which puts 2024 at +1.50 °C. NASA's own estimate against 1850 to 1900 is +1.47 °C.

Page 6 What's Causing It?

Where greenhouse gases come from, worldwide: electricity and heat 33%, transport 19%, industry 18%, farming and land use 13%, waste, leaks and other 12%, buildings 6%.

  • Climate Watch (World Resources Institute), via Our World in Data, Greenhouse gas emissions by sector primary source
  • Climate Watch primary source archived 2026-10-04

World total for 2023, about 52 billion tonnes of CO₂-equivalent. I grouped the dataset's categories. Transport includes aviation and shipping. Industry combines industry and manufacturing and construction. Waste, leaks and other combines waste, fugitive emissions (such as leaks from oil and gas), and other fuel combustion. Buildings is fuel burned on site, and electricity used in buildings is counted under electricity and heat. Rounded shares add to 101.

In the U.S., the heat wave season in major cities runs about 46 days longer than in the 1960s.

  • EPA, Climate Change Indicators: Heat Waves (archived copy, June 2025) primary source
  • EPA and NOAA data, Heat Wave Characteristics in 50 Large U.S. Cities, 1961 to 2023 primary source archived 2026-05-11

This compares the 2020s with the 1960s across 50 large metro areas. EPA removed its indicators website in December 2025, so the first link is an archived copy. The underlying data is still public.

Rain is arriving in more intense bursts, which can mean flash floods.

  • IPCC AR6 Working Group I, Summary for Policymakers (A.3.2 and B.2.4) primary source archived 2026-10-07

The IPCC finds heavy precipitation has become more frequent and intense over most land areas with good data, and that extreme daily rain intensifies about 7% per 1 °C of warming.

Extreme heat, drought, and flooding stress crops. Yields of major crops like corn and rice are expected to be lower than they'd otherwise be, which can push food prices up.

  • Zhao et al. (2017), Temperature increase reduces global yields of major crops, PNAS primary source
  • Tigchelaar et al. (2018), Future warming increases probability of globally synchronized maize production shocks, PNAS primary source archived 2025-07-22

Zhao et al. estimate that, without CO₂ fertilization, adaptation, or crop breeding, each 1 °C of global warming would cut global yields of maize by 7.4%, wheat by 6.0%, rice by 3.2%, and soybean by 3.1%. Tigchelaar et al. describe how grain production shocks drive price spikes.

Sea levels are up about 8 to 9 inches since 1880, from melting glaciers and ice sheets on land and warmer ocean water taking up more room.

  • Climate.us, Climate change: global sea level (NOAA's Climate.gov article, continued) primary source archived 2026-08-10
  • NASA, Sea Level indicator primary source archived 2026-10-01

NASA's page notes that about two-thirds of the rise comes from melting land ice and one-third from ocean warming. The address really does end in sea-leve.

Page 7 What Can Help?

The 2015 Paris Agreement aims to pursue efforts to limit warming to 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels.

  • UNFCCC, The Paris Agreement (Article 2) primary source

The goal refers to long-term warming over 20 or 30 years, not a single hot year.

  • United Nations, 1.5°C: what it means and why it matters (quoting WMO) primary source archived 2026-10-09
  • World Resources Institute, The 1.5 Degrees C Target secondary source archived 2026-09-04
  • Climate Analytics, Is the 1.5°C limit still in reach? secondary source archived 2026-07-29

Electricity and heat plus transport add up to just over half of all emissions.

  • Climate Watch via Our World in Data (see page 6) primary source

My calculation. 33% plus 19% is 52%.

Clean energy produces little to no greenhouse gases while it runs. Nuclear is clean but not renewable, since it runs on mined uranium.

  • EIA, Nuclear power and the environment primary source archived 2026-09-27
  • EIA Energy Kids, Uranium (nuclear) primary source archived 2026-09-29

EIA notes that reactors produce no carbon dioxide while operating, and that mining and refining uranium take a lot of energy. It classes uranium as nonrenewable.

Renewables supplied 33.8% of global electricity in 2025, ahead of coal at 33.0%.

  • Ember, Global Electricity Review 2026 primary source archived 2026-10-02

The cost of solar electricity is down 90% and onshore wind power is down 70% since 2010.

  • IRENA, Renewable Power Generation Costs in 2024 primary source archived 2026-08-01

IRENA's global weighted-average cost for utility-scale solar fell from $0.417 to $0.043 per kWh (2010 to 2024), and for onshore wind from $0.113 to $0.034.

The cost of battery storage is down 93% since 2010.

  • IRENA, Renewable Power Generation Costs in 2024 primary source archived 2026-08-01
  • BloombergNEF, battery pack prices fall to $108/kWh primary source archived 2026-10-02

IRENA measures utility-scale battery systems from 2010 to 2024. BloombergNEF's pack price survey reports the same 93% decline.

Page 8 Solar

Solar panels turn sunlight into electricity. Light knocks electrons loose in the silicon, an inverter converts the current into the kind a home uses, and extra goes back out to the grid.

  • EIA, Photovoltaics and electricity primary source
  • Department of Energy, Solar Integration: Inverters and Grid Services Basics primary source archived 2026-10-06

The diagram is my own drawing.

In the U.S., most solar power comes from big solar farms.

  • EIA, Wind and solar generated a record 17% of U.S. electricity in 2025 primary source archived 2026-09-17
  • EIA, Electricity Data Browser primary source archived 2026-10-03

My calculation. In 2025, utility-scale solar generated about 300 TWh and small-scale solar about 93 TWh, so about three-quarters came from large plants.

Solar made up more than half of all new generating capacity added to the U.S. grid in 2025.

  • SEIA and Wood Mackenzie, Solar Market Insight 2025 Year in Review primary source archived 2026-05-19
  • FERC, Energy Infrastructure Update, December 2025 primary source archived 2026-06-09
  • Electrek, FERC: renewables made up 88% of new U.S. power capacity in 2025 secondary source archived 2026-07-22

SEIA's 54% counts batteries in the total (solar and storage together were 79%). FERC's data, which counts only generators, puts solar above 72%. Either way, solar was more than half of new capacity.

Solar now produces just under 9% of U.S. electricity, up from about 1% a decade ago.

  • EIA, Electricity Data Browser primary source archived 2026-10-03
  • EIA, Wind and solar generated a record 17% of U.S. electricity in 2025 primary source archived 2026-09-17

My calculation from EIA data, counting rooftop (small-scale) solar. 2025 comes to 8.7% and 2015 to 0.95%.

Worldwide, 91% of new large-scale renewable projects in 2024 delivered power more cheaply than the cheapest new fossil alternative.

  • IRENA, Renewable Power Generation Costs in 2024 primary source archived 2026-08-01

IRENA's figure covers newly commissioned utility-scale (large-scale) projects, which is why the booklet says large-scale.

Battery storage costs have fallen 93% since 2010.

  • IRENA, Renewable Power Generation Costs in 2024 primary source archived 2026-08-01
  • BloombergNEF, battery pack prices fall to $108/kWh primary source archived 2026-10-02

Page 9 Wind

Wind turns the blades, the spinning rotates a generator, and power lines carry the electricity to the grid.

  • EIA, Wind explained primary source archived 2026-10-02
  • EIA, Electricity generation from wind primary source archived 2026-09-18

The diagram is my own drawing.

Texas built a 3,600-mile transmission network to carry wind power from rural areas to cities.

  • Rice University Baker Institute, Texas CREZ Lines: How Stakeholders Shape Major Energy Infrastructure Projects primary source archived 2026-09-11

This is the Competitive Renewable Energy Zones project, built between 2005 and 2014 at a cost of about $7 billion.

In the U.S., wind's share of electricity has more than doubled in ten years, from 5% in 2015 to 10% in 2025.

  • EIA, Electricity Data Browser primary source archived 2026-10-03
  • EIA, Wind and solar generated a record 17% of U.S. electricity in 2025 primary source archived 2026-09-17

My calculation from EIA data, with rooftop solar included in the total. 2015 comes to 4.66%. 2025 comes to 10.2% on current data, and 10.3% was the preliminary figure.

Some communities push back on wind farms near them.

  • Columbia Law School Sabin Center, Opposition to Renewable Energy Facilities in the United States (2026) primary source archived 2026-10-02

The Sabin Center finds wind projects are the most frequent target of local restrictions. The center also supports community groups that back renewable projects, so I read its counts with that in mind.

Page 10 Hydro & Geothermal

Hydro is the world's biggest renewable source at about 14% of global electricity.

  • Ember, World electricity data (updated May 2026) primary source archived 2026-10-02

Ember calls hydro the largest individual source of low-carbon electricity, at 14% in 2025.

In Norway, 99% of electricity comes from hydropower. Dams with large reservoirs can store water to meet peak demand.

  • IRENA, Hydropower primary source archived 2026-04-25

Geothermal plants run on hot water or steam within a mile or two of the surface.

  • EIA, Use of geothermal energy primary source archived 2026-10-03
  • EIA, Geothermal power plants primary source archived 2026-09-27

Kenya gets more than 40% of its electricity from geothermal.

  • EIA, Use of geothermal energy (Kenya at about 43% in 2022, the highest share of any country) primary source archived 2026-10-03
  • Stanford Understand Energy, Geothermal Energy (Kenya at 46%, 2024) secondary source archived 2026-09-08

The two sources cover different years. Kenya's share is above 40% in both.

Suitable sites for hydro are limited.

  • IEA, Hydropower Special Market Report, executive summary primary source archived 2026-10-01

The IEA ties the slowdown in new hydro to environmental concerns and fewer economically attractive sites.

Page 11 Nuclear

In a nuclear plant, atoms split in a process called fission and release heat, the heat boils water into steam, and the steam spins a turbine and generator.

  • Department of Energy, Nuclear 101: How Does a Nuclear Reactor Work? primary source archived 2026-10-12
  • EIA Energy Kids, Uranium (nuclear) primary source archived 2026-09-29

The diagram is my own drawing. The cooling tower label (just water vapor) follows EESI's description of nuclear plants.

Nuclear runs at full power more than 90% of the time.

  • EIA, U.S. nuclear industry (2025 capacity factor of 91%) primary source archived 2026-09-25

Nuclear supplies about 18% of U.S. electricity and more than 40% of its carbon-free power, still the largest single source. Both slipped slightly last year.

  • EIA, Electricity generation, capacity, and sales in the United States primary source archived 2026-10-02
  • EIA, Electricity Data Browser primary source archived 2026-10-03
  • MIT Climate Portal, Nuclear energy (over 40% of U.S. carbon-free electricity) primary source archived 2026-10-05

My calculation from EIA data. Nuclear was 17.8% of generation in 2024 and 17.4% in 2025. As a share of carbon-free generation (nuclear, hydro, wind, solar, geothermal) it was 43.5% and then 41.4%, so about 41 to 42% depending on what you count. Nuclear's 785 TWh in 2025 is more than wind (462), solar (393), or hydro (241).

At Chernobyl, 31 people died shortly after the explosion, and the long-term toll is still debated.

  • Idaho National Laboratory (DOE), Chernobyl: Just the Facts (2 killed by the explosion, 1 cardiac death, 28 from acute radiation syndrome) primary source archived 2025-04-08
  • World Health Organization, Radiation: The Chernobyl accident primary source archived 2026-09-18
  • WHO, Health effects of the Chernobyl accident: an overview (up to 4,000 additional cancer deaths among the most exposed) primary source archived 2026-10-11
  • Our World in Data, What was the death toll from Chernobyl and Fukushima? secondary source archived 2026-09-23

After Fukushima, more than 160,000 people moved away within weeks.

  • Our World in Data (see above) secondary source archived 2026-09-23
  • World Nuclear Association, Fukushima Daiichi accident secondary source archived 2026-10-01
  • World Nuclear Association, Fukushima: radiation exposure secondary source archived 2026-09-30

Counts vary with the definition. About 120,000 people were under official evacuation orders, and about 164,000 were displaced at the 2012 peak once voluntary moves are included.

Spent fuel stays radioactive for a very long time and is stored on-site.

  • EIA, The nuclear fuel cycle primary source archived 2026-10-05

Georgia's Vogtle reactors finished seven years late, at an estimated cost above $30 billion.

Page 12 Storage and the Grid

Solar and wind make power on nature's schedule.

  • EIA, Wind and solar generated a record 17% of U.S. electricity in 2025 primary source archived 2026-09-17

EIA describes wind and solar as intermittent, meaning they only produce when the wind or sun is available.

The grid is a network of power plants and wires and has to match supply and demand at every moment.

  • EIA, Delivery to consumers primary source archived 2026-10-04

A typical September day on the Texas grid, by source, with batteries charging below the line and releasing in the evening.

  • EIA, Hourly Electric Grid Monitor (ERCOT) primary source archived 2026-08-22
  • EIA, U.S. electric system is made up of interconnections and balancing authorities (ERCOT covers most, but not all, of Texas) primary source archived 2026-09-30

My averages. I took the mean for each local hour over September 1 to 21, 2026, converting EIA's UTC timestamps to Central daylight time. Negative battery values mean charging.

At 8 pm, batteries supplied about 11% of the grid's power, and gas supplies about half.

  • EIA, Hourly Electric Grid Monitor (same data) primary source archived 2026-08-22

My calculation from the same data. At 8 pm local time, batteries averaged 8.9 GW of about 81 GW of generation (11.1%), and gas was about 50%.

The world added roughly ten times as much battery capacity in 2025 as in 2021.

  • BloombergNEF, Energy Storage Enters the 100-Gigawatt Era primary source archived 2026-09-16
  • Canary Media, The world is installing grid batteries at a blistering pace secondary source archived 2026-08-19
  • pv magazine USA, Global battery additions reached 108 GW in 2025, according to IEA secondary source

BloombergNEF counts 112 GW in 2025, about tenfold the 2021 amount. The IEA counts 108 GW.

One in four new cars sold worldwide in 2025 was electric. Better, cheaper batteries are one reason.

  • IEA, Global EV Outlook 2026, Trends in electric cars primary source archived 2026-10-02

The IEA lists falling battery prices alongside charging infrastructure, supportive policy, and fuel prices.

Most grid batteries can deliver power for only a few hours at a stretch.

  • pv magazine USA, summarizing the IEA (new projects averaged about three hours in 2025) secondary source

Batteries depend on mined materials like lithium, and much of the world's battery manufacturing is concentrated in China.

  • BloombergNEF, battery pack prices fall to $108/kWh primary source archived 2026-10-02
  • Energy-Storage.News, Li-ion battery pack prices fell 8% (BloombergNEF) secondary source archived 2026-09-30

Page 13 Electrify Everything

Cars, SUVs, and pickups alone produce about one-sixth of all U.S. greenhouse gas emissions.

  • EPA, Transportation Sector Emissions primary source archived 2026-09-21
  • EPA, Technical Highlights, Transportation Emissions primary source archived 2026-02-19

My calculation. Transportation was 28% of U.S. emissions in 2022 and light-duty vehicles (cars, SUVs, pickups, minivans) were 57% of that, which is about 16%.

A gallon of gas weighs about 6 pounds, but burning it sends about 20 pounds of CO₂ out the tailpipe.

  • EPA, Greenhouse Gas Emissions from a Typical Passenger Vehicle primary source archived 2026-10-01

EPA's figure is 8,887 grams of CO₂ per gallon. Most of the weight comes from oxygen in the air.

Electric vehicles are far more energy efficient.

  • Department of Energy, fueleconomy.gov, All-Electric Vehicles primary source archived 2026-10-01

DOE says EVs convert over 77% of grid electricity to power at the wheels, while gasoline vehicles convert about 12 to 30% of the fuel's energy.

Even on today's average U.S. grid, a 2024 electric SUV has about 70% lower lifetime emissions than a gas SUV, battery included.

  • International Council on Clean Transportation, Why EVs are already much greener than combustion engine vehicles secondary source archived 2026-09-10
  • EPA, Electric Vehicle Myths primary source archived 2026-10-04

ICCT's modeled figure is 71% for an SUV. EPA agrees that EVs typically have a smaller lifetime footprint than gasoline cars.

EVs are quiet, smooth, and surprisingly fun to drive.

  • Department of Energy, fueleconomy.gov, All-Electric Vehicles primary source archived 2026-10-01

DOE lists quiet operation and strong acceleration. The word fun is mine. The line about used EVs is also my own view.

Chart: how much of the energy reaches the wheels (gas car 12 to 30%, electric car 77% or more) and how much heat you get for each unit of energy used (gas furnace 1 or less, heat pump 1.6 to 3).

  • Department of Energy, fueleconomy.gov primary source archived 2026-10-01
  • ENERGY STAR, Air-Source Heat Pumps (up to three times more heat than the electricity used) primary source archived 2026-10-02
  • PNNL, DOE Cold Climate Heat Pump Challenge field validation (median 1.6 to 2.7 below 30 °F) primary source archived 2026-09-13

A combustion furnace can't deliver more heat than the fuel contains, which is why it is shown as 1 or less.

Heating the house and the water are the biggest uses of energy at home, and nearly half of U.S. homes heat with natural gas.

  • EIA, Space heating and water heating account for nearly two thirds of U.S. home energy use primary source archived 2026-10-03
  • EIA, U.S. households' heating equipment choices (47% rely on natural gas) primary source archived 2026-09-26

Both come from EIA's 2015 household survey, the most recent detailed breakdown I found.

A heat pump doesn't make heat, it moves it from the outdoor air to the inside, and can deliver up to three times as much heat as the electricity it uses.

  • ENERGY STAR, Air-Source Heat Pumps primary source archived 2026-10-02

Water heaters, stoves, and dryers can be electrified as well.

  • Rewiring America, Electrify Everything in Your Home secondary source archived 2024-08-18

Rewiring America is an advocacy nonprofit, so I treat its guide as a how-to resource and not a neutral source for numbers.

Electrifying adds demand on the grid.

  • Ember, Global Electricity Review 2025 (EVs, heat pumps, and data centers adding to demand) primary source archived 2026-09-29

Page 14 How Do We Build It?

Big clean energy projects usually have to clear three gates: permission to build, a grid connection, and wires to carry the power. The gates can overlap and aren't always in this order.

  • Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Queued Up (the queue data covers projects connecting to the transmission grid) primary source

The three-gate framing is mine.

888 state laws and local ordinances restrict renewable siting, adopted across 48 states by the end of 2025.

  • Columbia Law School Sabin Center, Opposition to Renewable Energy Facilities in the United States (2026) primary source archived 2026-10-02

Restrictions include moratoria, bans, and rules like extreme setbacks that act as bans. The report notes that cancellations can't always be pinned on local opposition alone. The Sabin Center supports community groups that back renewable projects.

Only 13% of capacity that asked to connect between 2000 and 2020 has been built, and a project that does get built now typically takes over five years from request to operation.

  • Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Queued Up: 2026 Edition primary source
  • Gorman et al., Grid connection barriers to renewable energy deployment in the United States, Joule primary source

By the end of 2025, 75% of that capacity had been withdrawn and 10% was still active. The five-year figure is the median for projects built in 2025, in the regions with data. The queue is an indicator of demand to connect and not a measure of lost clean energy. Projects that face large grid-upgrade costs are more likely to withdraw.

About 350 miles of high-voltage lines were built per year from 2020 to 2023, down from about 1,700 per year in the early 2010s.

  • Grid Strategies and Americans for a Clean Energy Grid, Fewer New Miles (2024) secondary source archived 2026-05-23
  • Grid Strategies and Americans for a Clean Energy Grid, Fewer New Miles (2025) secondary source archived 2026-01-15

These are lines of 345 kV and above, based on FERC data, from an advocacy coalition and a consultancy. Single-year counts have been revised, so I use multi-year averages.

Who decides: towns, counties, and states for permission to build. Grid operators and FERC for a grid connection. States, FERC, and Congress for wires.

  • Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Queued Up (grid operators run the studies, FERC sets the rules) primary source
  • National Law Review, Senate permitting bill summary (state siting authority and FERC's backstop role) secondary source archived 2026-10-09

This is shorthand. Who decides varies by state and region.

Reforms are in the works.

  • Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Queued Up (FERC Order 2023 is being implemented) primary source
  • National Law Review, Senate permitting bill summary secondary source archived 2026-10-09

A bipartisan permitting bill was introduced in the Senate on September 30, 2026. Its status will change, so I'm not naming it in the booklet.

Find your U.S. senators and your state utility commission.

  • U.S. Senate, Contact your senators primary source archived 2026-10-02
  • NARUC, the national association of state utility regulators primary source archived 2026-10-06
